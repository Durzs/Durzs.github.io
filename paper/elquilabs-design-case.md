# ElquiLabs: Designing a Community-Centered Makerspace Network for Rural STEM Education in Chile's Elqui Valley

**A design case**

Authors: Diego Urzúa Escobar, Carlos Damián Zapata, Isidora Zavala Azócar, Thais Mendes, Zhang Yiwén
[AUTHORS: confirm final author order, full legal names, affiliations, ORCID iDs, and corresponding author]

## Abstract

This design case describes the conception and design of ElquiLabs, a network of community-centered makerspaces created to address the marginalization of STEM education in two rural communes of Chile's Coquimbo Region: Vicuña and La Higuera. Rather than intervening through schools, the design places permanently equipped, low-cost maker facilities inside existing community infrastructure — a municipal public library and a neighborhood association hall — and staffs them with locally recruited, trained supervisors. A hybrid digital platform extends the physical spaces with contextually themed courses, structured project challenges, and a socio-emotional learning strand. We describe the territorial and policy context that shaped the design, including the launch of the Elqui Local Public Education Service (SLEP); analyze three precedent interventions in the region whose trajectories directly informed our decisions; and narrate the principal design decisions, the alternatives we set aside, and the evidence and reasoning behind each choice. We close by discussing unresolved tensions — sustainability of funding, supervisor retention, connectivity constraints, and the risk that gamification displaces intrinsic motivation — and by outlining how we intend to judge whether the design works once implemented. The case is offered as a precedent for designers of informal STEM learning environments in rural Latin American contexts, where the dominant makerspace literature, produced largely in urban settings of the Global North, translates only partially.

**Keywords:** makerspace, rural education, STEM education, community-based learning, socio-emotional learning, Chile, design case

## 1. Introduction: The Design Challenge

The challenge we set ourselves was this: design an intervention that gives residents of two rural Chilean communes — children, but also adults and elders — durable access to STEM learning experiences, in a territory where such opportunities have historically arrived from outside, stayed briefly, and left.

The qualifier *durable* carries most of the weight in that sentence. As we document in Section 3, the recent history of STEM outreach in the Coquimbo Region is not a history of absence but of impermanence: well-received programs that visited rural schools and then discontinued, leaving behind enthusiasm and little infrastructure. The design problem, as we came to understand it, was therefore not primarily one of content delivery. It was one of institutional form: what kind of arrangement of spaces, people, artifacts, and incentives could keep STEM learning available in these communities after the initial funding cycle and the initial burst of attention have passed?

Our answer is ElquiLabs: a pair of permanently equipped makerspaces embedded in existing community venues, run by locally recruited supervisors, structured around twelve-week project challenges, and extended by a digital platform that combines locally themed STEM courses with an explicit socio-emotional learning (SEL) strand. This design case describes that answer as designed — the initiative has not yet been implemented — and, more importantly for readers of this journal, the reasoning, precedents, and trade-offs that produced it.

We write as the design team. In keeping with the design case genre (Boling, 2010), we aim for precision and honesty about our judgments rather than advocacy for the design, and we flag throughout the decisions we remain least certain about.

## 2. Design Context

### 2.1 Rural education and STEM in Chile

Chile's recent decades are usually narrated as an economic success story, but that growth has coexisted with persistent territorial inequality (Rojas & Charles-Leija, 2022; Mieres Brevis, 2020). Resources, research capacity, and innovation activity are concentrated in the Metropolitan Region; rural areas — particularly outside the capital's orbit — have been systematically disadvantaged by this centralization (Navarro & Álvarez, 2023). Rural poverty rates roughly double urban ones in national household survey data, and rural communities face marked barriers to accessing technology and innovation processes (Plaza et al., 2021).

The education system reproduces this pattern. Chile's schooling is deeply segmented between private and public provision, a structure with roots in the dismantling of the public apparatus during the military dictatorship (Ruiz Schneider, 2010). Rural public schools sit at the bottom of this hierarchy. The per-student voucher financing model disproportionately harms small rural schools, contributing to closures and to a chronic lack of recognition of rural specificity (Nuñez Muñoz et al., 2022). Educational policy is designed from an urban-centric perspective, and teachers arrive insufficiently prepared for multigrade, rural realities (Aliaga-Rojas & Del Pino, 2025; Williamson, 2024).

Within this already precarious provision, STEM education is marginal. Laboratories, materials, and specialized teachers are scarce; the interventions that do reach rural schools tend to be episodic outreach rather than sustained capacity (see Section 3). Yet the case for rural STEM capacity is strong precisely in these territories: STEM skills connect education to local productive vocations — agriculture, astronomy-linked tourism, renewable energy — and entrepreneurial ecosystems have proven fundamental to employment and poverty reduction where they take root (Modrego et al., 2021; Gavari-Starkie et al., 2024; Sedebo et al., 2025).

### 2.2 The territory: Vicuña, La Higuera, and a policy window

The two communes we designed for sit within the Elqui Province of the Coquimbo Region. **La Higuera** is an eminently rural commune with limited local educational offerings and productive vocations tied to fishing and small-scale agriculture. **Vicuña**, in the Elqui Valley proper, combines a small urban nucleus with extensive rural areas; its identity is bound to agriculture, astronomical tourism under some of the world's clearest skies, the legacy of Nobel laureate Gabriela Mistral — herself a rural schoolteacher of this valley — and the heritage of the Diaguita people.

A specific policy event shaped our timing. Chile is gradually transferring public schools from municipal administration to decentralized **Local Public Education Services (SLEPs)**, entities designed to manage public education with attention to each territory's needs (Anderson et al., 2023). The **SLEP Elqui**, which covers both Vicuña and La Higuera, began operating on July 1, 2025, taking responsibility for 112 educational establishments and an estimated 19,168 students (Ministerio de Educación, 2025). We judged the early years of this new service to be an unusually receptive period for complementary educational initiatives: administrative structures are being formed, territorial diagnosis is on the agenda, and the service's founding mandate emphasizes local pertinence. Designing *into* this window — rather than waiting to see how the SLEP consolidates — was itself a design decision (see Section 6.7).

## 3. Precedents: Three Interventions and What They Taught Us

Design cases benefit from making precedent explicit. Three prior STEM initiatives in the Coquimbo Region functioned for us the way precedent studies function in architecture: each contributed a lesson that is legible in the final design.

### 3.1 Itinerancias Rurales: the lesson of impermanence

Beginning in 2014, the "Itinerancias Rurales" program of PAR Explora Coquimbo brought science exhibits, talks, and workshops to rural schools, launching in Paihuano with roughly 200 students (PAR Explora Coquimbo, 2014; Martin Neira, 2015). The program was warmly received — and that reception is itself evidence that rural communities in this territory will engage with STEM opportunities when they arrive. But local press interviews from the period record teachers describing structural deficits the visits could not touch: minimal laboratory resources, little science instrumentation, and a desire to "develop more science in the field" that outlasted the program. Documentation of the program ceases after 2015.

For us the lesson was sharp: **itinerant models generate demand they cannot then serve**. An intervention that visits leaves nothing behind except raised expectations. This is the single strongest reason ElquiLabs is designed around permanent physical infrastructure rather than a mobile or event-based format.

### 3.2 OCRE: the lesson of sustained institutional anchoring

The Regional School Scientific Observatory (OCRE), run by the Universidad de La Serena, has operated for over six years, installing meteorological stations in schools and engaging students from seventh grade upward in collecting and analyzing local climate data. It grew from three schools to seventeen, urban and rural (Universidad de La Serena, 2025). Student testimony emphasizes exactly the outcomes we sought: sustained data practices, observation and analysis skills, and scientific identity formation.

OCRE demonstrated to us that **longevity is achievable in this region when a program is anchored to a durable institution and gives participants an ongoing productive role** (collecting real local data, not consuming demonstrations). Its limitation, from our perspective, is that it operates inside formal schooling and within one scientific domain. ElquiLabs borrows OCRE's anchoring logic — attach to institutions that persist — but anchors to *community* institutions and opens the activity space to community-defined projects.

### 3.3 Liga STEM: the lesson (and limits) of the engaging event

In May 2025, SLEP Puerto Cordillera organized a "Liga STEM" gathering over 500 students, including rural schools, for a day of challenges, workshops, and exhibitions (Pizarro, 2025). The format's strength is motivational: it makes STEM festive, social, and visible. Its structural limit is the same as any single event.

We incorporated this lesson not by rejecting the event format but by **repositioning it inside a longer arc**: each ElquiLabs project cycle culminates in a Community Innovation Fair (Section 5.3), so the motivating public event exists, but as the endpoint of twelve weeks of sustained work rather than as the intervention itself.

## 4. The Design Process

The design was developed by a five-person interdisciplinary team [AUTHORS: briefly state disciplinary backgrounds and how the team formed] working between [AUTHORS: dates]. Our process had three phases, which we report here because the structure of the analysis visibly shaped the structure of the design.

First, we conducted a **document-based territorial and causal analysis**, organized with a structured analytical toolkit (a 5W1H-based framework) that forced us to separate systemic causes of the rural STEM gap from context-specific ones. The systemic causes we identified were: (A1) geographic isolation and disconnection; (A2) centralization and historical neglect of rural territories; (A3) an inequitable, urban-centric educational structure; and (A4) a financing model detrimental to small rural schools. The context-specific causes were: (B1) inadequate rural educational provision with STEM marginalized within it; and (B2) restricted access to innovation and locally relevant skill development (Plaza et al., 2021; Modrego et al., 2021; Nuñez Muñoz et al., 2022).

Second, we carried out the **precedent analysis** reported in Section 3.

Third, we generated and iterated the design itself, requiring of every major component that it answer at least one identified cause, and checking each component against published evidence on makerspaces, rural STEM, and SEL. Table 1 (Section 6) preserves this cause-to-decision mapping, which we consider the intellectual core of the design.

Two honest caveats about process. The analysis was desk-based: at design time we had not yet conducted fieldwork or co-design sessions with residents of the two communes, a limitation we discuss in Section 7. ## 5. The Design

ElquiLabs is a hybrid initiative: two physical makerspaces plus a digital platform, operated through a cyclical program model and a deliberately local staffing structure.

### 5.1 Sites: existing community venues, not schools

The La Higuera makerspace is designed to be hosted in the **Municipal Public Library of La Higuera**; the Vicuña makerspace in the **Villa Diaguitas Neighborhood Association hall (Junta de Vecinos)** in the village of Diaguitas. Both are established, trusted congregation points with existing foot traffic, basic services, and — critically — institutional owners (a municipality, a legally constituted neighborhood association) with whom formal agreements can secure continuity of tenancy.

Choosing community venues over schools was among our most consequential decisions and is argued fully in Section 6.1. Its immediate design implication is the intended audience: ElquiLabs is intergenerational by design. The spaces are meant to host school-age youth alongside adults and elders — with elders positioned as carriers of traditional techniques and community memory, youth as contributors of digital fluency, and an explicit intention that women find room to lead. We designed for this mix rather than merely permitting it: workshop formats, scheduling, and the project themes described below all assume mixed-age groups.

### 5.2 The ElquiLabs Kit: a deliberately modest toolset

Each site is equipped with a standardized kit costing approximately **USD $1,980**: a consumer-grade 3D printer and filament stock, Arduino and micro:bit starter kits, a Raspberry Pi, an electronics components assortment, soldering stations, multimeters, hand tools, prototyping consumables, two refurbished laptops, safety gear, storage, a digital caliper, and a portable projector for group instruction. [AUTHORS: the full itemized budget table from the project brief can be included as an appendix if the editors find it useful.]

The kit's modesty is the point. We specify it in Section 6.2, but in brief: every item was selected to be replaceable from the project's operating budget, repairable or substitutable locally, usable by beginners after short training, and duplicable — so that expanding to a third site is a procurement task, not a design task.

### 5.3 The program model: twelve-week challenge cycles

Activity is organized in **twelve-week cycles**, each built around a publicly launched Challenge rooted in a local problem (an early example we developed: "design a technological solution to monitor water usage in local agriculture"). Within a cycle:

- **Weeks 1–2 — research and ideation.** Participants form project groups of three to five, balanced where possible across ages and skills; groups complete relevant short courses on the platform and draft a project plan.
- **Weeks 3–7 — prototyping.** Groups build in the makerspace with daily supervisor support and weekly check-ins.
- **Weeks 8–10 — testing and iteration.** Prototypes are tested with intended users and refined.
- **Weeks 11–12 — completion and showcase.** Groups finalize their work and present it at a **Community Innovation Fair** open to the community, municipal representatives, and local businesses.

Alongside cycles, structured **workshops** run at three levels: foundational (engineering design for 3D printing, basic electronics and circuit theory, computational thinking), applied (CAD with entry-level tools such as Tinkercad, soldering, precision hand-tool work), and thematic (e.g., environmental sensing, bio-inspired design, assistive technology), the last tier explicitly linking STEM disciplines to local contexts.

After completing a cycle, alumni gain **independent project access**: they may propose self-directed projects (goals, materials, timeline, expected impact) and, once approved, book the space and tools. This post-cycle pathway is our mechanism for converting program participants into resident users — the population that ultimately makes a community facility self-sustaining.

Illustrative challenge projects designed for the first cycles include a DIY telescope (optics and mechanical design, connecting to the valley's astronomical vocation), a portable solar-powered charger (renewable energy and basic electronics), and a small-scale hydroponic garden (plant biology, irrigation design, and water optimization — the region's defining constraint).

### 5.4 The people model: local supervisors, on-call mentors

Each site is run by **supervisors recruited from the communes themselves** — four to six people identified through municipal labor offices, community centers, and local networks — who complete a two-week intensive training: approximately 20 hours of hands-on technical mastery of the kit, 10 hours of pedagogy (project-based learning facilitation, group conflict resolution, cultivating a learning-from-failure mindset), 5 hours of SEL facilitation, and 5 hours of platform administration. Supervisors are compensated as part-time contractors for the duration of each cycle.

When a project group hits a problem beyond supervisor expertise — advanced statistics, complex circuit design — the supervisor files a mentor request through the platform, and the core team matches it to a pre-vetted **mentor** (drawn primarily from regional universities) for a targeted consultation, logged in the platform so the solution remains available to future groups.

The rationale for building the human layer this way, instead of deploying external facilitators, is developed in Section 6.3.

### 5.5 The digital platform

A mobile/web application extends the physical spaces with three functions:

1. **Locally themed courses.** Short online courses embed STEM objectives inside the valley's own referents — e.g., *Stargazing in the Elqui Valley* (celestial navigation, stellar lifecycles, telescope physics) and *Biodiversity of Elqui: A STEM Perspective* (local ecosystems, classification, sensor-based data collection). Courses are designed as supports for makerspace projects — theory, tool guides, inspiration — not as a standalone MOOC catalog.
2. **Challenge infrastructure.** Registration, group formation, challenge briefs, progress tracking, supervisor-moderated group channels, and the mentor request workflow.
3. **A personal growth strand** (Section 5.6).

We treat the platform as a complement to, never a substitute for, physical presence — a stance imposed partly by conviction and partly by rural connectivity realities (Section 7).

### 5.6 The well-being strand

Distinctively for a makerspace initiative, ElquiLabs integrates an explicit **socio-emotional learning component**: platform modules on mindfulness practices, emotional regulation, and self-compassion, plus supervisor training to facilitate brief SEL activities within makerspace sessions.

The pairing is not decorative. Making is emotionally demanding — prototypes fail, teams conflict, deadlines press — and we wanted the capacity to frame frustration and failure as material for growth built into the program rather than left to chance. The published evidence gave us confidence in the pairing from both directions: makerspace participation itself can contribute to young children's emotional development (Yang et al., 2025), and targeted SEL programs, including digitally delivered ones, have shown promise with rural student populations specifically (Ricker & Angosta, 2024). Section 6.5 discusses the decision and its risks.

### 5.7 Recognition: the ElquiPoints system

Participants earn symbolic points — **ElquiPoints** — for meaningful contribution: mentoring peers, sustaining participation, contributing to community initiatives. Points are redeemable for priority equipment access, additional mentoring sessions, prototyping kits, or seed support for new project ideas. We chose rewards that recirculate participants back into the makerspace rather than rewards external to it; Section 6.6 explains why, and Section 7 records our unresolved worry about gamification.

### 5.8 Sustainability arrangements (in brief)

Operational continuity rests on formal agreements with the two host institutions (including modest municipal contributions to utilities), a diversified funding strategy across corporate social responsibility programs of regional industries, joint grant applications with regional universities (Universidad de La Serena, Universidad Católica del Norte), and Chilean public funds for science outreach and social innovation (ANID Ciencia Pública, CORFO social innovation lines, the regional development fund FNDR). University alliances additionally supply interns as assistant supervisors and faculty as mentors. Once the SLEP Elqui consolidates, we intend to propose ElquiLabs as a formal extracurricular STEM provider for its schools. We keep this section brief deliberately: the financial engineering, while necessary, is not the design contribution of this case.

## 6. Key Design Decisions

This section narrates the decisions we consider most consequential, each with the alternatives we weighed and the reasoning — evidentiary and contextual — that settled it. Table 1 summarizes the mapping from diagnosed causes to design responses.

**Table 1.** *From diagnosed causes to design decisions*

| Diagnosed cause | Design response | Supporting evidence and reasoning |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| A1. Geographic isolation and disconnection | Permanent local physical spaces plus a digital platform that reduces distance to educational and collaborative resources | Makerspace models can effectively reach and serve rural youth (Kay & Buxton, 2024; Nixon et al., 2021); digital connectivity extends access where physical resources are distant |
| A2. Centralization and historical neglect | Community empowerment to define and solve local problems; local hiring; a local innovation hub that reverses the outward flow of talent and resources | Makerspaces can transfer agency to participants; local staffing retains program capacity in the territory |
| A3. Urban-centric educational structure | A complementary, flexible informal education model adapted to the two communes; valuation of diverse and traditional forms of knowledge | Makerspaces legitimate knowledge forms beyond the academic; informal provision can adapt where formal structures cannot |
| A4. Financing model harmful to small rural schools | Operation outside the school voucher system, with independent diversified funding | Complementary resources reach learners without competing for per-student vouchers |
| B1. Inadequate provision; STEM marginalized | Direct, continuous access to hands-on project-based STEM learning with integrated SEL | Makerspace participation supports STEM skills and identity in rural communities (Nixon et al., 2021); SEL supports the holistic development rural programs often omit (Ricker & Angosta, 2024; Yang et al., 2025) |
| B2. Restricted access to innovation and locally relevant skills | Learning-by-doing on locally defined problems; open project access; entrepreneur engagement | Rural entrepreneurial capacity depends on local spaces for experimentation (Modrego et al., 2021) |

### 6.1 Community venues rather than schools

*Alternatives considered:* hosting inside schools; a mobile/itinerant lab; building a dedicated facility.

We rejected the itinerant model on the direct evidence of the Itinerancias Rurales trajectory (Section 3.1). A dedicated facility was beyond any realistic budget and would take years to establish trust. Schools were the serious alternative — they offer captive audiences and alignment with curricula — but carried three costs we judged decisive: (a) school-hosted resources are effectively unavailable to adults, elders, and out-of-school youth, cutting off the intergenerational design; (b) school infrastructure in these communes is precisely the under-resourced, over-burdened system we were trying to complement, and adding an externally run program to it risked burdening teachers further (a deficit the precedent interviews made vivid); and (c) tying the initiative to individual schools would expose it to the administrative turbulence of the ongoing municipal-to-SLEP transition.

The library and the neighborhood association hall, by contrast, are stable, trusted, already social spaces whose institutional owners can sign continuity agreements. The cost of this choice is real: we forgo automatic access to students during school hours and must recruit participation actively. We accepted that cost.

### 6.2 A modest, replicable kit rather than a flagship fab lab

*Alternatives considered:* a high-end fabrication laboratory (laser cutter, CNC, professional printers); a purely low-tech craft space.

A flagship lab concentrates budget in equipment that is impressive but fragile in a rural setting: single points of failure, specialist maintenance dependencies hundreds of kilometers away, and intimidating to novices. A purely low-tech space, conversely, would not deliver the digital fabrication, physical computing, and programming experiences that constitute the actual STEM gap. The ~USD $1,980 kit sits deliberately between: consumer-grade tools with large user communities (hence abundant repair knowledge), beginner-appropriate entry points (micro:bit before Arduino before Raspberry Pi), and a unit cost low enough that the whole kit is duplicable for each new site and largely replaceable within an annual operating budget. Layout and equipment scale also matter for adoption in small rural facilities (Jiang et al., 2024), which reinforced our preference for compact, movable equipment over installed machinery.

### 6.3 Local supervisors rather than external facilitators

*Alternatives considered:* university outreach staff traveling from La Serena; volunteer-run operation.

External facilitation reproduces the visit-and-leave structure we designed against, and volunteers alone cannot guarantee the daily, reliable presence a permanent facility requires. Recruiting and paying supervisors from the communes does three things at once: it keeps program knowledge in the territory (if the program ends, the trained capacity remains); it models locally attainable STEM-linked employment; and it grounds the space's social legitimacy in familiar faces. The two-week training curriculum (technical, pedagogical, SEL, administrative) is our attempt to make this feasible with non-specialist recruits; whether two weeks is enough is one of our open questions (Section 7). Mentors on demand backstop the inevitable expertise limits.

### 6.4 Structured cycles rather than open drop-in access

*Alternatives considered:* a fully open workshop model where anyone builds anything anytime.

Open access is the romantic ideal of the maker movement, but the inclusivity literature warns that unstructured spaces tend to serve those who already possess maker identity and confidence (Andrews & Boklage, 2024). For a population with no prior makerspace exposure, structure is a scaffold, not a constraint: a public challenge lowers the "what would I even do here?" barrier; teams distribute risk; a fixed arc with a public fair supplies deadlines and an audience; and supervisors can actually be trained toward a known program. We preserved the open ideal as the *destination* rather than the entry point — alumni earn independent project access after a first cycle.

### 6.5 Integrating socio-emotional learning

*Alternatives considered:* omitting SEL; delegating well-being entirely to schools and families.

The argument against integration was focus: SEL is unusual in makerspace programs and stretches supervisor training. We kept it for three reasons. First, fit: iterative making constantly generates frustration, failure, and interpersonal friction, so the practices (emotional regulation, self-compassion) attach to genuine in-space experiences rather than abstract lessons. Second, evidence: makerspace participation and SEL outcomes appear mutually reinforcing (Yang et al., 2025), and digital SEL delivery has worked with rural students (Ricker & Angosta, 2024). Third, context: rural adolescents in these communes have thin access to psychosocial support generally; a community learning space that ignores this dimension wastes its position. The risk — supervisors delivering well-being content badly after five hours of training — is real, and we bounded it by keeping supervisor-led SEL to brief, scripted activities while placing the substantive modules on the platform.

### 6.6 Recognition through an internal credit economy

*Alternatives considered:* no reward system; monetary stipends for participants; conventional badges/leaderboards.

Paying participants was rejected as unsustainable and as framing participation as labor. Pure badge systems reward accumulation without directing behavior. ElquiPoints are earned disproportionately for *pro-social* contribution (mentoring peers, community projects) and redeemed for goods that deepen engagement (equipment priority, mentoring, seed support for new projects) — the economy is closed inside the learning community, so extrinsic rewards recirculate into participation rather than out of it. We nonetheless regard this as our most reversible decision and say so in Section 7.

### 6.7 Timing the launch to the SLEP transition

*Alternatives considered:* waiting for the new education service to consolidate before approaching it.

Launching alongside an administrative reform risks tying the project to institutional turbulence. We chose the window anyway: a founding-phase SLEP is actively seeking territorially pertinent initiatives, has no incumbent extracurricular providers, and its early agenda-setting will shape what "public education in Elqui" includes for years. Because the makerspaces are hosted by community institutions rather than schools (6.1), a turbulent SLEP transition degrades our upside (formal partnership) without threatening our base operation — an asymmetry we designed for deliberately.

## 7. Tensions, Risks, and Unresolved Issues

A design case owes readers its doubts. Ours are these:

**The design is not yet implemented.** Everything above is design reasoning, precedent, and published evidence — not observed outcomes. The strongest claims we can honestly make are about the *fit between diagnosis and design*, not about effects.

**We designed at a distance.** The territorial analysis was document-based. We did not run co-design sessions with residents of La Higuera or Diaguitas before fixing the design, which sits uneasily with a project whose rhetoric centers community agency. Our mitigation is procedural — the challenge themes, scheduling, and workshop content are deliberately underspecified so that the first cycles function as structured co-design — but we regard the absence of prior fieldwork as the design's most serious methodological weakness.

**Connectivity and device access.** The hybrid model assumes usable internet and some device access in communities where both are uneven. The physical spaces (with their laptops and projector) are the fallback, but if platform access effectively requires being in the makerspace, the "extended reach" argument for the app weakens considerably.

**Supervisor retention and depth.** Two weeks of training produces facilitators, not engineers. If cycle-based contracting cannot offer supervisors continuity, we will repeatedly retrain — recreating internally the impermanence we criticized externally.

**Gamification versus intrinsic motivation.** A sizeable literature warns that extrinsic reward systems can crowd out the intrinsic motivation making is supposed to cultivate. We designed ElquiPoints to recirculate engagement (6.6), but we hold the system lightly: it is the first component we would remove if early evidence shows point-chasing displacing project commitment.

**Cultural integration remains thin.** The design gestures at the valley's Diaguita heritage and Mistralian legacy, and positions elders as knowledge carriers, but the current curriculum operationalizes little of this. Deepening it — ideally with Diaguita community participation rather than about it — is committed but undone work.

**Funding dependency.** The diversified funding strategy (5.8) is a plan, not a portfolio. The precedents suggest the failure mode: Itinerancias Rurales ended, so far as public documentation shows, when its funding cycle did.

## 8. How We Will Judge the Design

Because the initiative is pre-implementation, we state here — as design intent, and as a commitment against which future reports can be checked — what we will observe:

- **Continuity indicators**, the design's raison d'être: sites operating at months 12/24/36; supervisor retention across cycles; proportion of alumni exercising independent project access.
- **Participation composition:** age distribution (testing the intergenerational claim), gender balance, and reach beyond the two host localities.
- **Learning and attitudes:** pre/post measures of STEM attitudes and self-efficacy per cycle with validated instruments adapted to Chilean Spanish; rubric-based assessment of project artifacts; platform learning analytics.
- **Socio-emotional outcomes:** validated SEL scales administered alongside the STEM measures, plus qualitative accounts of how (whether) the well-being strand figures in participants' experience.
- **Community embedding:** attendance at Innovation Fairs; count and nature of stakeholder engagements (sponsored challenges, mentor consultations, SLEP collaboration).

We intend to run the first implementation cycles as design-based research (Barab & Squire, 2004), treating each twelve-week cycle as an iteration that can revise the design — and to report those revisions, including the failures, in subsequent cases.

## 9. Reflection

Looking back over the design, its identity lies less in any single component — makerspaces, challenge cycles, SEL modules, and gamified credits each exist elsewhere — than in the consistency with which one criterion was applied: *does this component still work here after the visitors leave?* That criterion ruled out the itinerant format, chose the library over the school, put local residents rather than travelling experts in charge, priced the kit for replacement rather than for spectacle, and subordinated the app to the room. Whether it produces the durability it optimizes for is now an empirical question, and Section 8 is our promise to answer it in public.

For designers working on informal STEM learning in rural Latin America, we offer the case as a usable precedent in three specific ways: the cause-to-decision mapping (Table 1) as a transferable analytical device; the precedent-analysis move of reading *local intervention history* — not only the international literature — as design evidence; and the venue decision (6.1), which we suspect generalizes: in territories with weak formal institutions and strong community ones, build where the community already is.
