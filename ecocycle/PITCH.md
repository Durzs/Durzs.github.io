# EcoCycle — pitch kit

Three cuts of the same film, and what to say over them.

| Cut | Length | File | Use it when |
|-----|--------|------|-------------|
| Full | 5:04 | `EcoCycle_Collector_System.mp4` | Someone will watch it alone: judges reviewing submissions, a link in an email, the annex to the proposal. |
| **Pitch** | **3:00** | `EcoCycle_Collector_System_3min.mp4` | **You are in the room.** Play it silent and narrate live with the script below. |
| Short | 1:02 | `EcoCycle_Collector_System_1min.mp4` | Opening a stage pitch, a fair booth on loop, or a social post. |

All three play from the same page: `index.html` has a Full / Pitch / Short switch under
the timeline, or open it with `?cut=pitch3` / `?cut=short1`.

---

## What each cut prioritises

The film has three jobs: make the problem felt, make the mechanism legible, and prove the
economics. The cuts trade them off differently.

**The 3-minute cut keeps the mechanism whole.** All six steps survive at close to full
length — 78 of the 180 seconds are the six steps, because in a pitch the question that
kills a waste project is *"how would this actually run?"*, and that is the only part of
the film that answers it. The problem is compressed to 25 seconds (drain → fatberg →
flood), and the value gap keeps its full 15 seconds, because "cardboard at R$0.15 versus
oil at R$2.50" is the sentence that turns an environmental pitch into a poverty pitch.

What it gives up: the slow build of the opening, the depth of the drainage chemistry, the
retention and dashboard detail inside step six, and most of the recap.

**The 1-minute cut keeps the loop and the payoff.** Problem in 16 seconds, the collection
loop in 28, the numbers in 7. It drops step one (registration) and the "missing layer"
framing entirely: in 60 seconds nobody needs the onboarding, they need to see oil become
a verified batch and money come back. The bottle with the code still appears inside step
two, so the QR idea lands without a scene of its own.

## Live script — 3-minute cut

Play the video with no sound and speak these lines. Timecodes are where each line
*starts*; each one is written to fit the window before the next. Around 145 words a
minute — unhurried. **Do not read the on-screen captions aloud**; they say the same thing
in fewer words, so let them carry the audience while you add the emphasis.

> **0:00** — *(title)*
> EcoCycle collects used cooking oil in the Pavuna–Meriti basin of Rio.

> **0:05** — *(the drain)*
> Every day, thousands of small kitchens here fry food. When the oil is finished, nobody
> buys it, so it goes down the drain. One litre contaminates twenty-five thousand litres
> of water — and inside the pipes, it hardens.

> **0:21** — *(the flood)*
> That grease welds into blockages. Thirteen thousand unblocking operations a year in the
> Baixada. And when the rain comes, these streets flood.

> **0:30** — *(the value gap)* ← **the turn of the pitch; slow down here**
> Now the part that matters for poverty. The catadores who already work these streets live
> on cardboard, at fifteen to forty centavos a kilo. The oil flowing past them is worth up
> to two and a half reais a litre, and demand for it grew seven hundred percent in a decade.

> **0:45** — *(the missing layer)*
> The workers exist. The buyers exist. What is missing is the layer in between — something
> that collects the oil and proves what it is. That is our system. Six steps.

> **0:57** — *(step 1)*
> One: a shop registers at the residents' association and gets a PET bottle with its own
> code. No cost, no special container. Households can join too, for Green Points.

> **1:09** — *(step 2)*
> Two: a catador from the local cooperative collects at the door and scans that code. One
> scan captures four things — how much, from where, when, and who collected it. From that
> moment the oil is traceable, and the shop has a receipt.

> **1:27** — *(step 3)*
> Three: everything is pooled at a neighbourhood hub hosted by the residents' association,
> and tested for water and dirt. The batch only ships if the weight matches the sum of
> every scan. That is what stops oil from disappearing on the way.

> **1:44** — *(step 4)*
> Four: we sell the batch under contract, to several processors. PROVE, the earlier
> programme here, depended on a single refinery. When it was shut down in twenty
> twenty-six, the collected oil had nowhere to go. We do not repeat that.

> **1:58** — *(step 5)*
> Five: the revenue comes back by a rule fixed in advance. Forty percent to the catadores
> who collected it. Twenty to households, twenty as Green Points spent in local shops, and
> twenty to the Faetequinho course, which already trades free classes for used oil.

> **2:15** — *(step 6)*
> Six: every scan is data. The system forecasts how much each zone will produce, shortens
> the routes so collectors carry more per hour, and flags the shops about to drop out. Two
> dashboards — one for the city, one for buyers.

> **2:32** — *(the whole cycle)*
> Kitchen, scan, hub, contract, payment — and data that makes the next round cheaper. The
> same litre that used to block a pipe now pays a wage.

> **2:41** — *(the numbers)*
> In the Jardim América pilot: seventy-five thousand litres a year. Revenue above cost, and
> one twenty-seven of social value for every real invested. It pays for itself.

> **2:53** — *(close)*
> Same oil, a different path. **[the ask]** Thank you.

### The ask — pick one and rehearse it

- *Funding:* "We are looking for R$105,000 to run the Jardim América pilot for twelve months."
- *Partnership:* "We are looking for one processor and one municipality to sign the first contracts."
- *Programme:* "We are asking for a place in the accelerator, to turn the pilot into a basin-wide network."

### If you are cut to two minutes

Skip step 1 and the recap: jump the video from 0:57 to 1:09, and from 2:32 to 2:41. The
mechanism still reads, because step 2 shows the coded bottle anyway. Do not cut step 3 or
step 5 — verification is what makes the supply sellable, and the split is what makes it a
poverty intervention.

## Elevator script — 1-minute cut

> **0:00** EcoCycle.
> **0:02** Used cooking oil goes down the drain, hardens in the pipes, and blocks them.
> **0:08** Blocked pipes flood the same low-income streets, every season.
> **0:12** And that oil is worth several times the cardboard the catadores live on.
> **0:18** So: a catador collects it at the door and scans a coded bottle — how much, from where, when, who.
> **0:26** Pickups are pooled and tested at a neighbourhood hub into one verified batch.
> **0:32** Sold under contract to several processors, never just one.
> **0:37** Forty percent of the revenue goes to the catadores; the rest to households, local shops, and a school course.
> **0:44** Every scan sharpens the next round — forecasts, routes, retention.
> **0:50** The pilot recovers seventy-five thousand litres a year, pays for itself, and returns one twenty-seven in social value per real.
> **0:57** Same oil, a different path. EcoCycle.

**Three questions that will come, and the short answer:**

- *"What stops the oil being sold to informal buyers instead?"* — Instant credit on scan,
  plus the Green Points only spendable locally, plus the visible link to the Faetequinho
  course. A cash buyer can match the price; they cannot match the package.
- *"What if someone waters down the oil?"* — Visual check through the clear PET bottle at
  the door, density and hydrometer test at the hub, and every batch traced back to the
  wallet that supplied it, which can be frozen.
- *"Why will this reach the last mile when the existing programmes did not?"* — Because the
  collectors already live there. The drop-off networks ask residents to travel; we send
  recognized local collectors door to door, hosted by the residents' associations.
